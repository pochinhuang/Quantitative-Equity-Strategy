Quantitative Equity Research

Author

Brian Huang

Introduction

A large literature documents firm characteristics that predict stock returns, but the growing number of proposed factors has raised concerns about data mining and robustness. Jensen, Kelly, and Pedersen (JKP) systematically evaluate these factors and find that many remain significant after risk adjustment and multiple-testing corrections.

This project develops an end-to-end quantitative equity research process based on JKP’s framework. It first evaluates 153 equity factors through replication and a more recent sample, then uses these characteristics to construct alpha signals. The Quality Minus Junk (QMJ) strategy serves as an interpretable benchmark, while Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and XGBoost examine whether nonlinear models improve return prediction.

Finally, the alpha forecasts are combined with a Bloomberg fundamental factor risk model to construct risk-aware portfolios. Out-of-sample performance is evaluated using cumulative return, annualized return, volatility, Sharpe ratio, and maximum drawdown. The project therefore connects factor validation, alpha modeling, risk estimation, and portfolio construction within one systematic investment process.

Data

The analysis reconstructs the U.S. equity factor dataset from security level data rather than using precomputed factor returns as the primary input. Market data are obtained mainly from CRSP, accounting information from Compustat, and the CRSP/Compustat Merged database is used to connect the two sources. Additional data are used for currency conversion, risk free rates, market returns, and factor metadata.

Data Construction Overview

The raw datasets are combined in several stages to create a monthly stock panel that contains the information required to construct the 153 JKP characteristics.

CRSP monthly data provide the basic security market information, while CRSP Names data are used to identify the characteristics of each security at each point in time. The CCM linking table connects CRSP securities to Compustat firms. Compustat annual and quarterly fundamentals are standardized and then matched to the security panel using only accounting information that would have been available by each month end. CRSP daily data are used separately to construct characteristics that require higher frequency information.

The resulting monthly security dataset is then used to construct the 153 stock characteristics. These characteristics provide the inputs for the later predictive modeling and portfolio analysis. They are also used to sort stocks into portfolios, producing the 153 monthly factor return series used in the factor validation analysis.

Security Month Panel Construction

The first stage of the data construction creates a monthly panel in which each observation represents one CRSP security in one month. This panel provides the common structure onto which accounting information and stock characteristics are subsequently added.

The construction begins with the CRSP monthly stock file. Duplicate monthly security observations are removed, and CRSP trading dates are converted to calendar month-end dates. Stock price and shares outstanding are used to calculate security market equity,

\[ ME_{i,t} = |P_{i,t}| \times Shares_{i,t} \]

For firms with multiple securities, market equity is also aggregated at the company level using the CRSP company identifier (PERMCO).

CRSP Names data are then matched to the monthly observations using the historical period for which each name record is valid. This ensures that security information is measured as it existed at the corresponding point in time rather than using a firm’s current classification retrospectively. The resulting information is used to identify common stocks, primary U.S. exchanges, regular trading observations, and active securities. These conditions are combined to create the stock-universe eligibility indicator used later in factor construction.

The monthly Fama-French risk-free rate is also merged into the panel. Monthly excess stock returns are calculated as

\[ r^{exc}_{i,t} = r_{i,t}-r_{f,t} \]

CRSP identifies securities using PERMNO, whereas Compustat identifies firms using GVKEY. The CCM link history is therefore used to assign the appropriate Compustat identifier to each CRSP security. Link start and end dates are respected so that only links valid at the corresponding CRSP observation date are used.

Accounting information is prepared separately from Compustat Fundamentals Annual and Quarterly. The accounting datasets are standardized to create consistent definitions across annual and quarterly reports, and non-U.S.-dollar observations are converted to U.S. dollars where necessary.

To avoid using financial information before it would have been available to investors, each accounting report is assumed to become available four months after its fiscal period end. For each monthly security observation, the most recent annual and quarterly reports available by that month end are identified. When both are available, the more recent report is used, with the other source providing a fallback when a required variable is missing.

The result is a monthly security panel containing market information, security identifiers, eligibility indicators, excess returns, and the most recently available accounting information for each stock. This panel forms the base dataset for the construction of the 153 stock characteristics described in the next section.

Characteristic Construction

The monthly security panel is used to construct the 153 stock characteristics studied by Jensen, Kelly, and Pedersen. Rather than treating these characteristics as separate raw datasets, they are calculated from the market and accounting variables assembled in the previous section.

The characteristics are constructed at the individual stock level. Each observation therefore represents the value of a particular characteristic for a particular security at a particular month end. The construction follows the JKP definitions while preserving the point-in-time structure of the underlying data.

The 153 characteristics can be broadly divided into several groups.

Accounting Characteristics are constructed primarily from Compustat annual and quarterly fundamentals. These include measures related to profitability, investment, asset growth, accruals, leverage, financing activity, and financial strength. For example, profitability measures compare earnings or operating profits with assets or book equity, while investment measures capture changes in assets, capital expenditure, or inventories over time.

Valuation Characteristics combine accounting information with market equity. A simple example is the book-to-market ratio,

\[ BE/ME_{i,t} = \frac{\text{Book Equity}_{i,t}} {\text{Market Equity}_{i,t}} \]

Similar measures are constructed using assets, debt, sales, earnings, cash flow, and other accounting quantities relative to market value.

Return Characteristics are constructed from the historical return record of each security. These include momentum, short-term reversal, and seasonality measures calculated over different historical windows. Because these signals require prior returns, their availability depends on the amount of return history available for each stock.

Daily Market Characteristics use CRSP daily observations to capture information that cannot be measured accurately from monthly returns alone. These include measures of beta, volatility, idiosyncratic volatility, skewness, extreme daily returns, turnover, trading activity, and liquidity. Depending on the characteristic, the calculations use rolling daily windows such as approximately one month, six months, or one year.

Finally, several characteristics require additional combinations of accounting and market information or intermediate statistical calculations. These include measures such as Quality Minus Junk components, financial distress scores, mispricing measures, residual momentum, and other composite signals.

The different groups of characteristics are constructed separately and then merged by PERMNO and month end into a common security-month dataset. The final dataset contains all 153 JKP characteristics together with the stock identifiers, returns, market equity, and eligibility information required for subsequent analysis.

Conceptually, the resulting dataset has the following structure:

Security Month Market Data Accounting Data Characteristic 1 Characteristic 2 … Characteristic 153
Stock A Month \(t\) … … \(x_{1,A,t}\) \(x_{2,A,t}\) … \(x_{153,A,t}\)
Stock B Month \(t\) … … \(x_{1,B,t}\) \(x_{2,B,t}\) … \(x_{153,B,t}\)
Stock C Month \(t\) … … \(x_{1,C,t}\) \(x_{2,C,t}\) … \(x_{153,C,t}\)

Not every characteristic is available for every stock and month. Some measures require accounting information or several years of historical observations, while others require sufficient daily return history. When the required information is unavailable, the characteristic is left missing rather than being artificially filled. Factor portfolios are subsequently formed using the stocks with valid observations for each characteristic and month.

Factor Portfolio Construction

The 153 stock characteristics are converted into monthly factor returns using a common portfolio construction procedure based on Jensen, Kelly, and Pedersen. For each characteristic and each month, eligible U.S. stocks are sorted into characteristic portfolios using information available at the end of the formation month.

The procedure begins by identifying eligible stocks with positive market equity. Each month, the 20th and 80th percentiles of market equity are calculated using NYSE stocks. The NYSE 20th percentile is used to define the non-micro stock universe, while the NYSE 80th percentile is used to limit the influence of the largest firms in portfolio weighting.

For each characteristic, the tercile breakpoints are determined from the characteristic distribution among non-micro stocks. All eligible stocks, including micro stocks, are then assigned to the low, middle, or high tercile using these same breakpoints. This approach prevents very small stocks from determining the portfolio breakpoints while still allowing them to enter the final portfolios.

Portfolio returns are calculated using capped value weights. For stock \(i\) in month \(t\), the portfolio weight is proportional to

\[ w_{i,t} = \min \left( ME_{i,t}, P_{80,t}^{NYSE} \right) \]

where \(P_{80,t}^{NYSE}\) is the NYSE 80th percentile of market equity in that month. Stocks therefore receive larger weights when they have greater market capitalization, but the weight of very large firms is capped to prevent a small number of stocks from dominating the portfolio.

The characteristic signal observed at the end of month \(t\) is used to form portfolios whose returns are measured in month \(t+1\). The return of the high and low characteristic portfolios is therefore

\[ R^{High}_{j,t+1} = \sum_{i \in High_j} w_{i,t} r^{exc}_{i,t+1} \]

and

\[ R^{Low}_{j,t+1} = \sum_{i \in Low_j} w_{i,t} r^{exc}_{i,t+1} \]

The factor return is the difference between the high and low portfolio returns,

\[ f_{j,t+1} = d_j \left( R^{High}_{j,t+1} - R^{Low}_{j,t+1} \right) \]

where \(d_j\) is either \(+1\) or \(-1\) according to the direction associated with the factor in the original literature. This sign adjustment ensures that a positive factor return consistently represents the side of the characteristic associated with higher expected returns.

A factor return is recorded only when both the long and short portfolios contain at least five stocks. The same procedure is repeated for all 153 characteristics and all available months, producing the final monthly U.S. factor return dataset used in the factor validation analysis.

The resulting factor dataset has one observation for each factor and month:

Factor Month Direction Factor Return
Factor 1 Month \(t\) \(+1\) \(f_{1,t}\)
Factor 2 Month \(t\) \(-1\) \(f_{2,t}\)
… … … …
Factor 153 Month \(t\) \(+1\) \(f_{153,t}\)

These monthly factor returns are subsequently used to estimate CAPM alphas and evaluate factor replication under OLS, multiple-testing adjustments, and the empirical Bayes framework.

Sample Coverage and Timing

The final U.S. factor dataset contains all 153 characteristics and spans January 1950 through December 2025 at a monthly frequency. Because the data requirements differ across characteristics, individual factors do not necessarily begin in the same month. Characteristics that require accounting information or long return histories become available later than characteristics that rely on simpler market variables. The final dataset contains 130,769 valid factor-month observations across the 153 factors.

The timing of the analysis is designed so that portfolio formation uses only information available at the time of the investment decision. Characteristics measured at the end of month \(t\) are used to form portfolios for the return earned in month \(t+1\),

\[ X_{i,t} \longrightarrow R_{i,t+1} \]

Accounting information is incorporated only after the four-month reporting lag described above. This ensures that financial statement information is not used before it would have been available to investors. The same timing convention is maintained when the stock characteristics are later used as inputs to the predictive models.

Different parts of the analysis use different sample periods:

Analysis Sample Period Purpose
Full Sample Factor Validation 1950–2025 Apply the JKP validation framework and compare the results with those reported by JKP.
Modern Sample Validation 2000–2019 Evaluate whether the evidence for factor replication remains as strong in a common and more recent sample.
Predictive Model Development 2000-2014 Estimate and tune the stock return prediction models using data available before the out-of-sample portfolio period.
Out-of-Sample Portfolio Evaluation 2020–2025 Evaluate the investment strategies using observations not included in the initial model-development period.

For the time-series replication analysis, the in-sample period is defined separately for each factor using the sample dates reported in its original study. Observations before the beginning of that period form the pre-original sample, while observations after its end form the post-original sample. Factors are included in a comparison only when sufficient observations are available for both the original and out-of-sample periods.

This separation between the full historical sample, the modern validation period, and the later portfolio evaluation period allows the analysis to distinguish between long-run factor evidence, performance in more recent decades, and genuinely out-of-sample investment results.

Factor Validation

Jensen, Kelly, and Pedersen (JKP) study a broad universe of 153 equity factors and evaluate whether the return patterns documented in the asset-pricing literature can be replicated using a consistent empirical framework. Among these factors, 34 were not originally reported as having significant alpha, or were not evaluated in terms of factor return significance. JKP therefore define their headline replication rate using the remaining 119 factors that were reported as significant in the original literature.

This section first evaluates whether the main US factor-validation results in JKP can be reproduced using our independently constructed factor data. Factor performance is assessed using CAPM alpha, and statistical significance is evaluated under three approaches: ordinary least squares (OLS), the Benjamini–Yekutieli (BY) multiple testing adjustment, and an empirical Bayes (EB) framework. These methods provide increasingly structured ways to account for estimation uncertainty and the large number of factors being tested simultaneously.

After replicating the full sample US results, we examine several dimensions of robustness, including differences across factor themes, stock size groups, and out-of-sample periods. Finally, the same validation framework is applied to the 2000–2019 period to assess whether the evidence for factor replicability remains as strong in a more recent market environment.

Validation Methodology

Following JKP, factor performance is evaluated using CAPM alpha rather than raw returns. Statistical significance is assessed using three approaches: ordinary least squares (OLS), the Benjamini–Yekutieli (BY) multiple-testing adjustment, and an empirical Bayes (EB) model. The full available US history through 2020 is used for the main replication analysis.

Ordinary Least Squares

For each factor, CAPM alpha is estimated from

\[ f_{i,t} = \alpha_i + \beta_i r_{m,t} + \varepsilon_{i,t} \]

where \(f_{i,t}\) is the factor return, \(r_{m,t}\) is the market excess return, and \(\alpha_i\) measures the factor’s abnormal return after controlling for market exposure.

OLS tests

\[ H_0:\alpha_i=0 \]

A factor is classified as replicated when its estimated alpha is positive and statistically significant at the 5% level.

Benjamini–Yekutieli Adjustment

Testing many factors simultaneously increases the risk of false discoveries. The Benjamini–Yekutieli (BY) procedure adjusts the OLS significance levels to control the false discovery rate while allowing the individual tests to be dependent.

A factor is classified as replicated under BY when

\[ \widehat{\alpha}_i>0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad p_i^{BY}<0.05 \]

Because the BY adjustment imposes a stricter significance threshold than individual OLS tests, fewer factors are generally classified as replicated.

Empirical Bayes

The empirical Bayes framework estimates factor alphas jointly rather than treating each factor independently. Noisy alpha estimates are shrunk toward zero while information is shared among factors belonging to the same economic cluster.

For the US-only model,

\[ \widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}} \mid \boldsymbol{\alpha} \sim \mathcal{N} \left( \boldsymbol{\alpha}, \Sigma \right) \]

with the prior

\[ \boldsymbol{\alpha} \sim \mathcal{N} \left( \mathbf{0}, \Theta \right) \]

where

\[ \Theta = \tau_w^2 I + \tau_c^2MM^\top \]

Here, \(M\) represents factor-cluster membership, \(\tau_c^2\) captures variation across cluster-level alphas, and \(\tau_w^2\) captures factor-specific variation within clusters. The posterior mean is

\[ \boldsymbol{\mu}_{\mathrm{post}} = \Theta (\Theta+\Sigma)^{-1} \widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}} \]

A factor is classified as replicated under EB when the lower bound of its 95% posterior interval is greater than zero.

Replication Rate

The full set of 153 factors is used when estimating the validation models. However, JKP identify 34 factors for which the original study either did not report a significant alpha or did not evaluate factor-return significance. These factors are excluded from the headline replication rate, leaving 119 originally significant factors.

The replication rate is therefore defined as

\[ \text{Replication Rate} = \frac{ \#\{\text{originally significant factors that replicate}\} }{ 119 } \]

The same OLS, BY, and EB framework is later applied to the 2000–2019 subsample to examine whether replication evidence remains as strong in a more recent market environment.

Replication of JKP Results

We first examine whether the main US factor-validation results reported by JKP can be reproduced using our independently constructed factor returns. The analysis uses each factor’s full available US history through 2020. Replication rates are evaluated among the 119 factors that were reported as significant in the original literature.

Figure 1 compares factor-level CAPM alpha estimates under OLS, the Benjamini–Yekutieli (BY) multiple-testing adjustment, and the empirical Bayes (EB) framework. Each point represents the estimated monthly alpha of an individual factor, while the vertical intervals reflect estimation uncertainty. Factors are classified according to whether they were significant in the original literature and whether they remain significant under each validation method.

Under OLS, most originally significant factors continue to exhibit positive and statistically significant CAPM alpha. The BY adjustment leaves the OLS alpha estimates unchanged but applies a stricter significance threshold to account for multiple testing, resulting in fewer replicated factors. The EB approach instead adjusts both the estimated alpha and its uncertainty by pooling information across related factors and shrinking noisy estimates toward zero and their cluster-level component.

Our replication rates are very close to those reported by JKP. OLS produces a replication rate of 81.5%, compared with 82.4% in JKP. After the BY multiple-testing adjustment, the rate declines to 73.1%, compared with 75.6% in the original study. The EB estimate returns to 84.0%, again close to JKP’s 82.4%.

Method JKP This Study
OLS 82.4% 81.5%
BY 75.6% 73.1%
EB 82.4% 84.0%

The differences are less than one percentage point across all three methods, indicating that the main US internal-validity results in JKP can be closely reproduced using our independently constructed factor data.

Cross Sectional Robustness

After establishing that the aggregate US replication results closely match JKP, we next examine whether the evidence is robust across different groups of factors and stocks. JKP organize the factor universe using hierarchical agglomerative clustering based on correlations among CAPM-residual factor returns. The resulting clusters are intended to capture groups of factors with similar statistical behavior and economic interpretation.

Factor Clusters

Our clustering procedure also produces 13 groups. These labels are descriptive rather than exact replications of the JKP theme names, since some factors are assigned differently in our clustering results.

Replication Across Stock Size Groups

A potential concern is that factor replication may be driven primarily by small or illiquid stocks, where factor effects may be stronger. To examine this possibility, we reconstruct the factors separately within five market-capitalization groups and apply the same empirical Bayes replication criterion.

Figure 2 shows that replication remains substantial across most size groups. The replication rates are 73.1% for mega-cap stocks, 79.8% for large stocks, 85.7% for small stocks, and 84.9% for micro stocks. The nano-stock sample has the lowest replication rate at 63.0%.

The pattern is broadly consistent with JKP. Their corresponding replication rates are 77.3%, 79.8%, 85.7%, 85.7%, and 68.1% for mega, large, small, micro, and nano stocks, respectively. In both studies, replication remains high among large and mega stocks rather than being confined to small or micro stocks, while the nano-stock sample shows noticeably weaker evidence.

Overall, these results suggest that factor replication is not solely a small- or micro-cap phenomenon, although the strength of the evidence varies across the size distribution.

Replication Across Factor Clusters

We next examine whether factor replication is concentrated in only a few types of factors. Figure 3 reports the empirical Bayes replication rate for each of the 13 factor clusters introduced above.

Replication rates differ substantially across clusters. Clusters 13, 8, and 4 have the highest replication rates, while Clusters 1 and 3 have the lowest. Based on the approximate interpretations in Table 1, these two weaker groups are mainly related to Low Leverage / Balance Sheet Strength and Size / Liquidity.

A similar pattern appears in JKP. Low Leverage and Size are the only two of their 13 themes with replication rates below 50%, while the other 11 themes have replication rates of at least 50%. Although our cluster assignments do not exactly match those in JKP, the weaker performance of similar groups is broadly consistent with the original study.

Together, Figure 2 and Figure 3 show that factor replication is not limited to a particular stock size or a small set of factor types. However, the strength of replication varies across both stock size and factor category.

Clusters Alpha Across Stock Size Groups

The previous two analyses examine stock size and factor clusters separately. We next consider both dimensions together by comparing the average alpha of each cluster across the five stock size groups. This allows us to examine not only whether factors replicate, but also whether the magnitude and ranking of factor themes remain similar across different segments of the stock market.

For each size group, Figure 4 reports the average OLS alpha for each of the 13 factor clusters.

The results show some similarities in factor performance across stock size groups, although the ordering of the clusters is less consistent than in JKP. The Spearman rank correlation between the cluster alphas of mega and micro stocks is 50.0%, while the correlation between mega and nano stocks is 15.4%.

JKP report corresponding correlations of 73% for mega versus micro stocks and 36% for mega versus nano stocks. Their results therefore show greater consistency across stock sizes than ours. Nevertheless, both analyses show the same general pattern: the rankings for mega and micro stocks are more similar, while nano stocks differ more substantially from the larger stock groups.

These results indicate that factor alphas vary across different parts of the market. In particular, the relative importance of the 13 factor clusters changes with stock size, with the largest differences appearing among nano stocks. At the same time, the results in Figure 2 show that substantial factor replication remains present across most stock size groups.

Time Series External Validity

The previous analyses use the full available sample to evaluate factor replication. We next examine whether factor performance persists outside the periods originally studied in the literature. For each factor, the original study period is treated as the in sample period, while observations before and after that period are treated as out of sample.

Figure 5 compares each factor’s in sample CAPM alpha with its out of sample alpha. The first panel uses observations before the original study period, the second uses observations after the original study period, and the third combines both periods.

The relationship between in sample and out of sample performance is positive in all three cases. Before the original sample, 79 of 92 factors have positive out of sample alpha, and the GLS slope is 0.61 with a t statistic of 3.80. After the original sample, 99 of 117 factors have positive alpha, with a slope of 0.25 and a t statistic of 3.87. When the periods before and after the original sample are combined, 106 of 117 factors have positive alpha, and the estimated slope is 0.30 with a t statistic of 4.19.

All three slopes are significantly positive, indicating that factors with higher alpha during the original study period also tend to have higher alpha outside that period. However, the slopes are well below one, showing that the strength of factor performance declines outside the original sample. The decline is particularly clear after the original study period, where the slope falls from 0.61 before the sample to 0.25 after the sample.

These results closely resemble the pattern reported by JKP, who estimate GLS slopes of 0.57 before the original sample, 0.26 after the original sample, and 0.35 when both periods are combined. JKP also document a decline in average alpha from 0.49% per month in sample to 0.26% after the original sample, corresponding to a 47% reduction.

Overall, the evidence suggests that factor performance persists out of sample, but with meaningful attenuation. The positive relationship between in sample and out of sample alpha is inconsistent with a setting in which the original results are entirely driven by data mining, while the lower slopes indicate that the magnitude of the original factor performance does not fully persist.

Modern Sample Validation

The previous analyses focus on reproducing the main results in JKP using the full available US sample. As an extension, we apply the same OLS, BY, and empirical Bayes validation procedures to a common 2000–2019 sample. This provides a direct comparison of factor replication in a more recent market environment while keeping the validation framework unchanged.

Figure 6 compares the replication rates from the full sample with those obtained using only observations from 2000 through 2019.

Replication rates decline under all three methods. Under OLS, the rate falls from 81.5% in the full sample to 55.5% in 2000–2019, with 66 of the 119 originally significant factors remaining significant. The decline is substantially larger after the BY adjustment, from 73.1% to 20.2%, corresponding to 24 of 119 factors. Under empirical Bayes, the replication rate decreases from 84.0% to 37.8%, with 45 of 119 factors classified as replicated.

The empirical Bayes estimates also change in the more recent sample. The estimated cluster dispersion parameter, \(\tau_c\), decreases from 0.3231% per month in the full sample to 0.2135%, while the within cluster dispersion parameter, \(\tau_w\), decreases from 0.1886% to 0.1580%. The smaller dispersion estimates imply stronger shrinkage of uncertain alpha estimates toward zero, contributing to the lower empirical Bayes replication rate.

These results indicate that the statistical evidence for factor replication is materially weaker during 2000–2019. However, the decline should not be interpreted entirely as evidence that the factors have disappeared. The modern sample contains only 20 years of monthly observations, compared with the much longer histories available for many factors in the full sample. A shorter sample increases estimation uncertainty and lowers statistical power, which is particularly important for the more conservative BY procedure.

Therefore, the modern sample results are consistent with weaker factor evidence in recent decades, but they also reflect the greater difficulty of establishing statistical significance over a shorter period. Together with the out of sample results above, the evidence suggests that many factor effects persist beyond their original study periods, although their statistical strength and estimated magnitude are substantially weaker in the more recent sample.

Alpha Models

This section develops two types of alpha signals. QMJ provides a transparent rule based benchmark, while machine learning models use firm characteristics to estimate the probability that a stock’s next month excess return will exceed the cross sectional median.

QMJ Alpha Model

Following Asness, Frazzini, and Pedersen (2019), firm quality is defined using three dimensions: profitability, growth, and safety. QMJ provides a transparent rule-based benchmark in which firms with stronger fundamentals receive higher quality scores, which are used as alpha signals in this project.

Each month, characteristic \(x_{i,t}\) is ranked across stocks and standardized:

\[ z_t(x_{i,t}) = \frac{ \operatorname{rank}_t(x_{i,t})-\mu_{r,t} }{ \sigma_{r,t} } \]

where \(\mu_{r,t}\) and \(\sigma_{r,t}\) are the cross-sectional mean and standard deviation of the ranks.

Profitability combines gross profits over assets (GPOA), return on equity (ROE), return on assets (ROA), cash flow over assets (CFOA), gross margin (GMAR), and low accruals (ACC):

\[ \mathrm{PROF}_{i,t} = z_t\left[ \frac{1}{6} \left( z_{\mathrm{GPOA},i,t} + z_{\mathrm{ROE},i,t} + z_{\mathrm{ROA},i,t} + z_{\mathrm{CFOA},i,t} + z_{\mathrm{GMAR},i,t} + z_{\mathrm{ACC},i,t} \right) \right] \]

Growth measures the five-year growth in the corresponding residual per-share profitability measures, excluding accruals:

\[ \mathrm{GROW}_{i,t} = z_t\left[ \frac{1}{5} \left( z_{\Delta_5\mathrm{GPOA},i,t} + z_{\Delta_5\mathrm{ROE},i,t} + z_{\Delta_5\mathrm{ROA},i,t} + z_{\Delta_5\mathrm{CFOA},i,t} + z_{\Delta_5\mathrm{GMAR},i,t} \right) \right] \]

Safety combines low market beta (BAB), low leverage (LEV), low bankruptcy risk based on the O-Score and Z-Score, and low ROE volatility (EVOL):

\[ \mathrm{SAFE}_{i,t} = z_t\left[ \frac{1}{5} \left( z_{\mathrm{BAB},i,t} + z_{\mathrm{LEV},i,t} + z_{\mathrm{O},i,t} + z_{\mathrm{Z},i,t} + z_{\mathrm{EVOL},i,t} \right) \right] \]

All safety measures are signed so that higher values represent safer firms. The three components are equally weighted to produce the overall quality score:

\[ Q_{i,t} = z_t\left[ \frac{ \mathrm{PROF}_{i,t} + \mathrm{GROW}_{i,t} + \mathrm{SAFE}_{i,t} }{3} \right] \]

When an individual measure is unavailable, the remaining available measures are averaged. A higher \(Q_{i,t}\) indicates a more profitable, growing, and financially safe firm.

In the original paper, stocks are first divided into small and large cap groups and then sorted by quality. The resulting portfolios are value weighted and rebalanced monthly. The QMJ factor is long high quality stocks and short low quality, or junk, stocks within both size groups:

\[ QMJ_t = \frac{1}{2} \left( R_{SQ,t}+R_{BQ,t} \right) - \frac{1}{2} \left( R_{SJ,t}+R_{BJ,t} \right) \]

where \(S\) and \(B\) denote small and big stocks, while \(Q\) and \(J\) denote quality and junk stocks.

For this project, the stock-level quality score is used directly as the rule-based alpha signal:

\[ s^{\mathrm{QMJ}}_{i,t} = Q_{i,t} \]

Stocks with higher QMJ scores are assigned higher expected-return rankings for subsequent portfolio construction.

Machine Learning Alpha Models

Prediction Target

The prediction target, \(Y^{1M}_{i,t}\), indicates whether stock \(i\)’s next month excess return exceeds the cross sectional median return in month \(t\):

\[ Y^{1M}_{i,t} = \begin{cases} 1, & r_{i,t+1} > \operatorname{Median}_{j\in\mathcal{U}_t} \left(r_{j,t+1}\right) \\[6pt] 0, & r_{i,t+1} \leq \operatorname{Median}_{j\in\mathcal{U}_t} \left(r_{j,t+1}\right) \end{cases} \]

where \(r_{i,t+1}\) is stock \(i\)’s next-month excess return and \(\mathcal{U}_t\) is the set of stocks with available returns in month \(t\).

Using stock characteristics \(X_{i,t}\), each model estimates

\[ \widehat{p}_{i,t} = P\left( Y^{1M}_{i,t}=1 \mid X_{i,t} \right) \]

The predicted probability \(\widehat{p}_{i,t}\) is subsequently used as the stock level alpha score. Since the target is defined relative to the monthly median, the two classes are approximately balanced by construction.

Feature Selection

To reduce redundancy among the 153 JKP characteristics, feature selection was performed using observations from 2000–2009. A random sample of 350,000 stock-month observations was used, and missing values were replaced with the corresponding training-sample medians.

The characteristics were grouped using complete-linkage hierarchical clustering. The distance between characteristics \(j\) and \(k\) was defined as

\[ d_{jk}=1-\left|\rho_{jk}\right| \]

where \(\rho_{jk}\) is their Pearson correlation. Therefore, characteristics with strong positive or negative correlations were placed in the same cluster.

The hierarchy was divided into 100 clusters, and the characteristic with the highest variance within each cluster was retained as its representative. This reduced the feature set from 153 to 100 characteristics.

As shown in Figure 7, retaining 100 features preserves an average \(R^2\) of 0.9137, or approximately 91.4% of the information in the original feature set. Subsequent models are estimated using both the full 153-feature set and the selected 100-feature subset.

Machine Learning Models

Three classification models are considered: Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and XGBoost. Logistic Regression provides a transparent linear benchmark, while Random Forest captures nonlinear relationships and interactions by combining multiple decision trees. XGBoost builds trees sequentially to improve prediction accuracy.

Each model is trained using both the full set of 153 characteristics and the selected subset of 100 characteristics. Hyperparameters are selected through time-series cross-validation. The predicted probability of outperforming the monthly median is subsequently used as the stock-level alpha score.

Cross Validation and Hyperparameter Tuning

An expanding window time series cross validation procedure was used to preserve the chronological order of the data and prevent future information from entering the training sample. The five folds were constructed as follows:

Fold Training Period Validation Period
1 Jan. 2000–Nov. 2009 Jan.–Dec. 2010
2 Jan. 2000–Nov. 2010 Jan.–Dec. 2011
3 Jan. 2000–Nov. 2011 Jan.–Dec. 2012
4 Jan. 2000–Nov. 2012 Jan.–Dec. 2013
5 Jan. 2000–Nov. 2013 Jan.–Dec. 2014

A one month gap was maintained between each training and validation window. Hyperparameter tuning was conducted separately for the full 153 features set and the selected 100 features subset. ROC AUC was used as the primary model selection criterion, while accuracy and log loss were also reported.

A two stage tuning procedure was applied. The first stage searched over a broad parameter range, while the second stage conducted a narrower search around the best-performing region.

  • Logistic Regression: The first stage tested both L1 and L2 penalties with C values from 0.001 to 10. The refined stage focused on the L1 penalty and smaller C values between 0.0001 and 0.003.

  • Random Forest: The first stage varied the number of trees, maximum tree depth, and number of features considered at each split. The refined stage focused on 300–500 trees, maximum depths of 15–20, and square-root feature sampling.

  • XGBoost: The first stage searched across the number of trees, maximum tree depth, and learning rate. The refined ranges were selected separately for the full and selected feature sets based on the first-stage results.

The parameter combination with the highest average validation ROC-AUC across the five folds was selected for each model and feature set.

Risk Model

Factor Risk Model

Following the Bloomberg Global Equity Fundamental Factor Model, stock returns are decomposed into systematic factor returns and idiosyncratic returns. The covariance matrix of stock returns is estimated as

\[ \Omega = X F X^{\top} + \Delta \]

where:

  • \(\Omega\) is the \(N \times N\) stock return covariance matrix;
  • \(X\) is the \(N \times K\) factor exposure matrix;
  • \(F\) is the \(K \times K\) factor return covariance matrix;
  • \(\Delta\) is the idiosyncratic covariance matrix.

The idiosyncratic covariance matrix is assumed to be diagonal, with each diagonal element representing the idiosyncratic variance of one stock. Under this structure, portfolio variance can be decomposed into systematic and idiosyncratic risk:

\[ w^{\top}\Omega w = w^{\top}X F X^{\top}w + w^{\top}\Delta w \]

Factor Covariance Matrix

The factor covariance matrix is decomposed into factor volatilities and factor correlations:

\[ F = V C V \]

where \(V\) is a diagonal matrix of factor volatilities and \(C\) is the factor correlation matrix. Following Bloomberg factor volatilities are estimated using an exponentially weighted moving average with a 26 week half-life.

The factor correlation matrix combines an exponentially weighted historical correlation matrix with a PCA correlation matrix:

\[ C = 0.8C_{0} + 0.2C_{P} \]

where \(C_{0}\) is estimated using an exponentially weighted moving average with a 12 month half-life, while \(C_{P}\) is constructed from the principal components associated with the largest eigenvalues. This combination retains economically meaningful historical relationships while reducing estimation noise.

Portfolio Optimization

At the end of each month, the alpha signal and estimated covariance matrix are used to determine portfolio weights. The optimization problem is

\[ \max_{w} \left\{ \alpha^{\top}w - \frac{\lambda}{2} w^{\top}\Omega w \right\} \]

where \(\alpha\) is the vector of QMJ or machine-learning alpha scores, \(w\) is the vector of portfolio weights, and \(\lambda\) is the risk-aversion parameter.

The first term rewards exposure to stocks with higher alpha scores, while the second term penalizes predicted portfolio risk. A larger value of \(\lambda\) produces a more risk controlled portfolio. The optimization is subject to the relevant budget, long-short exposure, and maximum position constraints of each strategy.

Backtest

Probability Weighted Portfolios

At the end of each month \(t\), stocks are ranked by the predicted probability \(\hat{p}_{i,t}\) that stock \(i\) will earn a positive excess return in month \(t+1\). Probability weighting uses both the ranking and magnitude of these predictions, assigning larger weights to stocks associated with stronger model confidence.

These portfolios do not use a covariance matrix or risk penalty. They therefore serve as a baseline for evaluating the economic value of the machine learning signals before incorporating the factor risk model.

Long-Only: Top 10%

The long-only strategy selects the 10% of stocks with the highest predicted probabilities. Let \(\mathcal{L}_t^{10}\) denote this set:

\[ \mathcal{L}_t^{10} = \left\{ i: \hat{p}_{i,t} \geq Q_{0.90,t} \right\} \]

where \(Q_{0.90,t}\) is the 90th percentile of predicted probabilities in month \(t\). For each selected stock, the portfolio weight is:

\[ w_{i,t} = \frac{ \hat{p}_{i,t} }{ \sum_{j\in\mathcal{L}_t^{10}} \hat{p}_{j,t} }, \qquad i\in\mathcal{L}_t^{10}. \]

The portfolio is fully invested and long-only:

\[ \sum_i w_{i,t}=1, \qquad w_{i,t}\geq 0. \]

The portfolio formed at the end of month \(t\) is evaluated using realized excess returns in month \(t+1\):

\[ R_{t+1} = \sum_i w_{i,t}r_{i,t+1}. \]

Figure 8 and Table 2 show that the six model specifications produce relatively similar long-only performance. Logistic Subset achieves the strongest result, with an annualized return of 10.68%, a Sharpe ratio of 0.646, and a cumulative return of 73.26%. Overall, the long-only portfolios generate positive returns, but their risk-adjusted performance remains moderate.

Long-Short: Top/Bottom 5%

The long-short strategy buys the 5% of stocks with the highest predicted probabilities and shorts the 5% with the lowest predicted probabilities. The two sets are defined as:

\[ \mathcal{L}_t^{5} = \left\{ i: \hat{p}_{i,t} \geq Q_{0.95,t} \right\} \qquad \mathcal{S}_t^{5} = \left\{ i: \hat{p}_{i,t} \leq Q_{0.05,t} \right\} \]

Within the long leg, weights are proportional to \(\hat{p}_{i,t}\). Within the short leg, the confidence score is defined as \(1-\hat{p}_{i,t}\), so stocks with lower predicted probabilities receive larger absolute short weights:

\[ w_{i,t} = \begin{cases} \displaystyle 0.5 \frac{ \hat{p}_{i,t} }{ \sum_{j\in\mathcal{L}_t^{5}} \hat{p}_{j,t} }, & i\in\mathcal{L}_t^{5} \\[14pt] \displaystyle -0.5 \frac{ 1-\hat{p}_{i,t} }{ \sum_{j\in\mathcal{S}_t^{5}} (1-\hat{p}_{j,t}) }, & i\in\mathcal{S}_t^{5} \end{cases} \]

The portfolio allocates 50% gross exposure to each leg, resulting in zero net exposure and 100% total gross exposure:

\[ \sum_i w_{i,t}=0 \qquad \sum_i |w_{i,t}|=1 \]

Its realized return is:

\[ R_{t+1} = \sum_i w_{i,t}r_{i,t+1} = 0.5 \left( R_{t+1}^{\mathrm{Long}} - R_{t+1}^{\mathrm{Short}} \right) \]

where \(R_{t+1}^{\mathrm{Short}}\) denotes the realized return of the stocks in the short basket before applying the negative portfolio exposure.

Figure 9 and Table 3 show that the long-short portfolios substantially outperform their long-only counterparts, although they also experience deeper drawdowns. Random Forest Subset produces the highest annualized return of 31.53% and cumulative return of 441.39%, while XGBoost Full achieves the highest Sharpe ratio of 1.436. Random Forest Full records the smallest maximum drawdown among the six long-short portfolios at -31.42%.

Risk Optimized Portfolios

The probability weighted portfolios use only the machine learning predictions to determine portfolio weights. This section incorporates a factor risk model so that portfolio construction considers both predicted alpha and portfolio risk.

Optimization and Backtest Setup

The predicted probabilities from each machine learning model are treated as cross sectional alpha scores. Portfolio weights are obtained by solving:

\[ w^* = \underset{w}{\operatorname{arg\,max}} \left\{ \alpha^\top w - \frac{\lambda}{2}w^\top\Omega w \right\} \]

The first term rewards exposure to stronger alpha signals, while the second penalizes predicted portfolio variance. The optimization is evaluated using:

\[ \lambda \in \{0,100,300,1000\} \]

When \(\lambda=0\), portfolio construction depends only on the alpha signal and portfolio constraints. Larger values of \(\lambda\) place greater emphasis on risk control.

For the long-short strategy, the top and bottom 5% of stocks ranked by predicted probability form the long and short candidate sets. Each stock is subject to a maximum absolute weight of 1%.

At the end of month \(t\), alpha forecasts, risk estimates, and portfolio weights are calculated using only information available at that time. Performance is then evaluated using realized returns in month \(t+1\).

Backtest Results

The risk model backtest is conducted using XGBoost Full and Random Forest Sub. QMJ serves as the primary long-short benchmark, while the S&P 500 is included as a market performance reference. All return series cover the same evaluation period and are normalized to an initial value of 1.

Random Forest Sub

Figure 10 and Table 4 show a clear trade off between return and risk. At \(\lambda=0\), the portfolio achieves the highest annualized return of 53.20% and Sharpe ratio of 1.609, but also exhibits 33.07% volatility and a maximum drawdown of -56.38%.

As \(\lambda\) increases to 1000, annualized volatility declines to 20.97% and maximum drawdown improves to -45.97%. However, annualized return also falls to 14.56% and the Sharpe ratio declines to 0.694. Among the risk-managed specifications, \(\lambda=300\) provides a middle ground, producing a 28.19% annualized return and a Sharpe ratio of 1.116, although its drawdown remains substantially larger than that of the S&P 500.

XGBoost Full

Figure 11 and Table 5 indicate stronger overall performance for the XGBoost Full signal. The \(\lambda=0\) portfolio generates the highest annualized return of 74.12% and Sharpe ratio of 2.163, accompanied by 34.27% volatility and a maximum drawdown of -49.86%.

At \(\lambda=300\), annualized volatility falls to 23.81%, while the portfolio retains an annualized return of 32.97% and a Sharpe ratio of 1.385. By contrast, the \(\lambda=1000\) portfolio produces performance close to the S&P 500, suggesting that a very large risk penalty removes much of the return advantage provided by the alpha signal.

Overall, XGBoost Full produces higher returns and Sharpe ratios than Random Forest Sub at each corresponding value of \(\lambda\). The results also demonstrate that stronger risk penalization reduces portfolio volatility, but this improvement comes at the cost of lower returns. In this comparison, \(\lambda=300\) provides a reasonable balance between preserving the alpha signal and controlling portfolio risk.
